What’s new

Every time the maths behind this tool changes, the version at the bottom of the page changes with it. It is worth knowing which one you were looking at, because two answers produced by different versions are not directly comparable.

v1.41  ·  2 Oct 2026

Two tranches of the same deal now share one fate

If you bought into the same deal twice, the model was treating the two rows as two different companies. Each drew its own outcome, so a single simulated future could write one tranche off entirely and pay the other its sponsor case. That cannot happen: one company has one fate. It also quietly manufactured diversification that does not exist, so two $10,000 tranches of one deal looked safer than one $20,000 position in it.

Rows are grouped by the deal they resolve to, not by name, so Eephus Growth Fund I and II stay independent as they should while two rows of the same fund do not. If your book has tranches, your numbers will move — the downside gets slightly worse and the upside slightly better, because the false averaging is gone. A book without tranches is unchanged to the cent.

What is NOT shared is the exit year. That comes from each row's own dates, and overruling what you typed is not this tool's job. But one company only exits once, so if two rows of the same deal carry different exit years the page now says so and tells you to make them match. That usually happens when you filled the exit columns on one row and left them blank on the other, which makes the tool derive a different year from year funded plus hold.

v1.40  ·  2 Oct 2026

A capital call you dated was being ignored by the preferred return

Found by Kristin Westergard on her own book, and she was right to the dollar. Her Asilia Evergreen row is $400,000 committed, nothing funded, and she had told the tool the next call is Q1 2027. The ladder still showed $37,333 of income for 2026. It should have been $26,000, which is the yield on uncalled capital, because in 2026 none of it has been called.

Why. v1.38 added the Next call date column and taught the capital-calls panel about it. It did not teach the preferred return, which kept its own copy of the old assumption that a commitment is drawn evenly over three years from the year after you funded. So the calls table said nothing arrives before 2027 while the coupon was being paid on a third of the commitment in 2026. The $37,333 was $20,000 of coupon on money that had not been called, plus $17,333 of yield on the rest. There is now one schedule, used by both, so they cannot disagree again.

Zero is no longer shown as a loss. A deal whose typical outcome is “you get your capital back” has a rate of return of exactly zero, and the solver lands a hair underneath it, so the table printed a red −0.0%. On a book with several single venture deals that is most of the column. Anything inside half a basis point now reads 0.0% in plain ink. The genuinely negative figures are unaffected, and a sleeve can show a small negative: that column is a yearly rate, so −0.4% a year is a few per cent of capital over a hold, not a loss bigger than what you put in.

And the by-deal table keeps your order. It used to sort by sponsor case and throw away the order you typed, which splits two tranches of the same deal apart and makes them hard to compare. Your tracker order is now the default, with buttons to re-sort by sponsor case, typical return or commitment.

You do not have to download anything. The columns are optional and the app works exactly as it always has without them, so an existing tracker keeps producing the figures it produced yesterday. This is only for people who want to start dating their calls.

If you do, you have two ways in and neither needs a migration: add the two columns to the sheet you already have — headed Next call date and Next call amount, anywhere in the row, since columns are matched by name and not by position — or download the new TRACKER v1.8 and move your rows across. v1.38  ·  1 Oct 2026

Tell it when your next capital call is due, and it stops guessing

Two optional columns: Next call date and Next call amount. Leave them blank and nothing changes. Fill them in and the tool uses what you know instead of what it assumed. Sponsors rarely publish a call schedule, so until now it took whatever was still uncalled and spread it evenly over about three years from the year after you funded. That is a reasonable guess and it is still the fallback, but it was being asserted over people who had better information, which is the opposite of how the rest of this works.

Any spelling of the date works — 2027, Q1 2027, 2027-03, March 2027 — because only the year is used. This calendar is annual, and holding the quarter would imply a precision nothing downstream uses. With a date and an amount, that amount lands in that year and the remainder spreads after it. With the date alone, nothing is shown arriving before it, which is usually the thing you actually want to say.

An amount with no date is refused, out loud. There is no year to put it in, and choosing one would invent exactly the thing the column exists to stop. Those rows fall back to the even spread and the panel names them, because a column you filled in that quietly did nothing is worse than no column at all.

The panel also lists which positions came from your tracker rather than from the model, and echoes your date back as you wrote it so you can see it was read correctly. A year you have dated yourself no longer shows the still ahead estimate, since pro-rating a known date by months elapsed would be the tool second-guessing the one person who knows.

Under the hood: this panel had grown its own second copy of the call schedule, so the rule lived in two files and could drift without either looking wrong. It now calls the same function the simulation does.

v1.37  ·  1 Oct 2026

Three more from Kristin Westergard, all about reading the screen

The gold preferred-return lines on the ladder now carry their amount, which the exit blocks have had since the chart shipped. Hover either. The figure was always being calculated, it was just never attached to the one element that arrives on a promised schedule rather than on a hope.

The capital-calls year now says what it is. Asked whether it meant what has been called so far or what is left, and the honest answer is neither. Sponsors rarely publish a call schedule, so the tool takes whatever is still uncalled and spreads it evenly over about three years from the year after you funded. v1.36  ·  1 Oct 2026

“>1000%” was not a return. It was the solver giving up.

Found by Kristin Westergard, and it is the most consequential thing anyone has reported. The by-deal table on Performance & Returns showed a good run of >1000% against the Asilia Credit Fund Evergreen. Read as written, that says the fund has an extraordinary upside and no downside. It was not a return at all. It was the bisection solver hitting the top of its search range and the table printing the range.

Why it happened, and who else saw it. When most of a commitment is still uncalled, the preferred return starts arriving while the capital is still in your account. So cash comes back before the rest of it goes out, and there is no single annual rate that describes that shape — mathematically there can be several, or none below 1000%. On a test position of $400,000 committed against $20,000 funded, 19% of runs could not be solved at all. This was never specific to ACFE: any position paying a preferred return with a large uncalled balance could do it, which on a typical Growth Circle book also means the Asilia GC Fund and BlueBird Capital.

The engine always knew. It has labelled these runs internally since the rate of return was added, and nothing ever displayed the label. A run it could not solve was counted and then quietly folded in with the ones it could.

What changes. A rate that cannot be solved is now reported as unsolved rather than as the ceiling, and the unsolved runs keep their place at the top of the ordering instead of being deleted from it — deleting them is the quieter version of the same error, because the runs that break are the highest ones, so dropping them hands you a complete-looking band built only from the runs that behaved. So the good run now reads n/a exactly when a tenth or more of the runs are unsolvable, while the typical column is unaffected. The table now names the deals involved, the share of runs, and the reason, and points you at the multiple on capital, which is unaffected and is the more honest measure for a part-funded position anyway: a high rate on money you have not sent yet is not a return you can spend.

If your positions are fully called, not one figure moves. That was checked rather than assumed.

A yield on uncalled money can now run out

Austin Read at Asilia answered the question Kristin Westergard put to him, and the answer was more specific than anyone expected. Uncalled commitments in ACFE do earn before any capital is called, at roughly 6–7% annualised rather than the 4% first reported. v1.24  ·  28 Sep 2026

A preferred return is earned on money the fund has actually called

Kristin Westergard found this one. She asked whether the tool pays the headline rate on your whole commitment while most of it is still uncalled. It did. For a position you have funded in full this changes nothing and always did. For one still being drawn down it was the most generous of every possible reading.

On the example book that is Asilia GC Fund — $100,000 committed against $19,000 funded — and it was worth $9,720 of preferred return that had not been earned yet. v1.17  ·  26 Sep 2026

A preferred return is no longer a certainty

Until now the model paid every preferred return in full, in every single one of the 6,000 simulated futures. That was the most optimistic assumption in the tool and it made the income sleeves look steadier than they are. A member asked whether the bad scenarios should not also pay less, which is the right question.

How much arrives now depends on how the deal turned out — and on the same draw as the exit, not a separate one. That detail is the whole point: the fund that hands back less than plan is the same fund that stopped paying its coupon. Drawing the two independently would have let a position lose its entire equity while still paying seven years of preferred return, which does not happen. A position that writes off its equity pays about a quarter of its scheduled preferred return; one well below plan pays about 70%; one near plan about 90%.

What it does to the example book. The documents promise $231,000 of preferred return. The typical future now collects $197,400, and a bad run collects $166,200. The income sleeve’s typical yearly return falls from 19.6% to 18.1% and the whole book from 12.4% to 11.8%. Venture does not move at all, because it pays no preferred return — which is a useful check that the change did what it was meant to and nothing else.

The gap between income and venture narrowed but did not close, and that is worth knowing: the gap was never mostly about this. It is mostly that money back sooner is worth more to a yearly rate, and that a single venture deal carries a 30% chance of returning nothing against 5% for an income sleeve. There is now an explanation of that on the returns panel, because it is the first question the table invites.

The shares are judgements rather than findings, and they sit in one place in the code so they can be argued with. Thanks to the member who pushed on this one.

v1.16  ·  26 Sep 2026

IRR, and a column for what you think a position is worth now

There is an IRR panel on Liquidity Outlook — by deal, by asset class, and across the whole book. Two things about it are worth knowing before you read it.

It counts cash, not marks. Every dollar in it is money actually returned, so it will read LOWER than an IRR a sponsor reports, because theirs includes unrealised carrying value. That gap is the difference between the two methods, not an error in either.

A portfolio IRR is not the average of its deals’ IRRs. IRR is not additive. The panel pools every position’s cash flow, solves once per simulated future, and ranks the futures after that. On the example book, averaging the thirteen deals instead gives a median of −5.4% against the correct 12.4% — which is the difference between a healthy book and a loss-making one, so it is worth saying out loud.

The most useful number turned out to be one nobody asked for. Every single venture SPV in the example book has a roughly 30% chance of returning nothing and a median IRR of 0.0%. Pooled as a sleeve, the same eight positions have a median of 7.2% and a P10 of −0.8%. Nothing about any deal changed, only that there are eight of them.

A sponsor’s multiple is now shown as its own IRR. v1.1  ·  24 Sep 2026

Funds are no longer treated like single deals

A fund that holds twenty or thirty companies behaves nothing like a single company, and the first version priced them the same way. The old maths gave every venture position a 30% chance of returning nothing. For one company that is fair. For a fund of twenty it would take every company in it failing at once, which has never happened to a real fund.

Funds now use their own set of odds, taken from what venture funds have actually returned rather than from anything invented here. A useful side effect: it puts the sponsor’s own target multiple at roughly the top 5% of outcomes, which is where a target belongs. Something to aim at, not the expected result.

Funds pay out over several years, not all on one day

A fund sells its holdings gradually, so the cash arrives over a stretch of years. The tool used to drop the whole lot into a single year, which for something meant to be a calendar was the bigger mistake of the two. Anything holding more than one deal now spreads its payout across the years around its exit.

This moves no money. It only puts it in the right years, and your lifetime totals come out exactly the same either way.

What you need to do

There is a new column in the tracker called Deals in fund. Put 1 there, or leave it empty, and nothing changes from before. Put the real number in and your funds stop being priced like single companies. If you are not sure how many the fund holds, ask the sponsor.

A single company being bought is still one event on one date, so leave those at 1.
