Private Deals

Everything you hold in private markets, and when it might pay you back. The Liquidity Outlook tab is the Exit Timing Calendar, now part of a bigger tool. It will still not give you a predicted exit date, because nobody has one.

v1.17  ·  26 Sep 2026

A preferred return is no longer a certainty

Until now the model paid every preferred return in full, in every single one of the 6,000 simulated futures. That was the most optimistic assumption in the tool and it made the income sleeves look steadier than they are. A member asked whether the bad scenarios should not also pay less, which is the right question.

How much arrives now depends on how the deal turned out — and on the same draw as the exit, not a separate one. That detail is the whole point: the fund that hands back less than plan is the same fund that stopped paying its coupon. Drawing the two independently would have let a position lose its entire equity while still paying seven years of preferred return, which does not happen. A position that writes off its equity pays about a quarter of its scheduled preferred return; one well below plan pays about 70%; one near plan about 90%.

What it does to the example book. The documents promise $231,000 of preferred return. The typical future now collects $197,400, and a bad run collects $166,200. The income sleeve’s typical yearly return falls from 19.6% to 18.1% and the whole book from 12.4% to 11.8%. Venture does not move at all, because it pays no preferred return — which is a useful check that the change did what it was meant to and nothing else.

The gap between income and venture narrowed but did not close, and that is worth knowing: the gap was never mostly about this. It is mostly that money back sooner is worth more to a yearly rate, and that a single venture deal carries a 30% chance of returning nothing against 5% for an income sleeve. There is now an explanation of that on the returns panel, because it is the first question the table invites.

The shares are judgements rather than findings, and they sit in one place in the code so they can be argued with. Thanks to the member who pushed on this one.

v1.16  ·  26 Sep 2026

IRR, and a column for what you think a position is worth now

There is an IRR panel on Liquidity Outlook — by deal, by asset class, and across the whole book. Two things about it are worth knowing before you read it.

It counts cash, not marks. Every dollar in it is money actually returned, so it will read LOWER than an IRR a sponsor reports, because theirs includes unrealised carrying value. That gap is the difference between the two methods, not an error in either.

A portfolio IRR is not the average of its deals’ IRRs. IRR is not additive. The panel pools every position’s cash flow, solves once per simulated future, and ranks the futures after that. On the example book, averaging the thirteen deals instead gives a median of −5.4% against the correct 12.4% — which is the difference between a healthy book and a loss-making one, so it is worth saying out loud.

The most useful number turned out to be one nobody asked for. Every single venture SPV in the example book has a roughly 30% chance of returning nothing and a median IRR of 0.0%. Pooled as a sleeve, the same eight positions have a median of 7.2% and a P10 of −0.8%. Nothing about any deal changed, only that there are eight of them.

A sponsor’s multiple is now shown as its own IRR. The outcome table puts a sponsor’s figure in the top 10% of outcomes only, so at the median and even at a good run it was invisible: four deals with sponsor multiples between 5.5x and 14.2x were printing an identical number. The sponsor case now sits beside the model’s own percentiles so the two can be compared directly.

New optional column: Marked Up MOIC. If you track valuation increases — your position doubling when a round reprices — you can record it. It drives a paper track shown beside the cash forecast and never inside it: it moves no cash figure, and it never enters the IRR. Blank means nothing changes, and a tracker from before this version produces identical numbers to the ones it produced yesterday.

Thanks to Rick Strauss for asking for IRR by investment, asset class and scenario, and to John Millen for the markup argument — his reason for tracking paper gains was better than the reason for ignoring them.

v1.12  ·  25 Sep 2026

BlueBird rebuilt from its PPM, and what it actually is

Every number was already right, which is worth saying: 15% preferred, a 7-year term, a 2.65x target, 2% management fee, $100k minimum. The documents confirmed the card rather than correcting it.

The description of the deal was wrong, though. The card said “a single operating company, not a pool”. It is a roll-up: it buys several small gutter and roofing businesses across multiple markets, combines them into one operation, and sells that platform to a private equity or strategic buyer. It buys many and sells one — which is still why it is modelled as a single exit rather than a fund selling down, but for a different reason than the card gave.

The waterfall is now written out in full, and it is worth reading. 15% preferred first, then 100% of your capital back, then carried interest of 90% to the GP and 10% to you. The sponsor takes nothing until your capital and the whole accrued preferred are paid, which is investor-friendly — but above that line the split is heavily theirs, so a realistic outcome sits close to capital plus preferred. Read the 2.65x target with that in mind.

Fund term is 7 years, extendable by the GP for two further one-year periods.

v1.11  ·  25 Sep 2026

Both Eephus funds rebuilt from their PPMs, and the class ladders are real now

The documents arrived, so the guessing stops. Your Eephus class is set by how much you invested, exactly as was suspected, and the tool now works it out from your commitment and applies the right preferred rate by itself.

Fund I — $50k buys Class A-4 at 6%, $100k gets A-3 at 7%, $250k gets A-2 at 8%, $500k gets A-1 at 8%.

Fund II is not the same ladder, which is worth knowing if you are in both. It has five classes and pays more at the top: $50k is A-5 at 6%, $100k is A-4 at 7%, $250k is A-3 at 8%, $500k is A-2 at 8%, and $1M is A-1 at 9%.

Also corrected: the management fee is 2% of the income the properties generate, not 2% of your committed capital. Those are very different things and the card said the wrong one. Carry on Fund I is 30% to the sponsor after the preferred, then a 20% ARR hurdle, then 50/50; Fund II runs a 30% catch-up, then 70/30 to a 15% IRR, then 50/50.

Your fee class is now worked out from your commitment

v1.8  ·  25 Sep 2026

Three reading bugs, found by pointing a new test suite at the spreadsheet

None of these could be seen on screen. The page rendered normally in every case, which is what made them worth finding.

A percentage typed as text was read as a whole number. If your preferred return cell contained the text 8% rather than a number formatted as a percentage, it was read as 8 — a preferred return of 800% a year. Excel usually hides this, because a percent-formatted cell really holds 0.08. A CSV does not: export that same cell and it is written out as the text “8%”. That is the route anyone on a Mac was told to take, so this only ever hit the people least able to spot it. If your book showed absurd income, this was why. Reload and drop your file again.

A column headed “Type” lost its asset class. When the Fund / Single deal column arrived in v1.5 it claimed “Type” as one of its names, and took it off the asset-class column, which had used it since the beginning. Trackers predating v1.5 were the ones affected — the people who had been using the tool longest.

A column headed “Funded year” was read as an amount. It starts with the word “funded”, so it was matched to funded-to-date: a year of 2026 became $2,026 paid in, and the position then showed an uncalled balance that did not exist.

Also: the tracker now carries its own version number, separate from the app’s. They were the same number, so a release that changed no columns was telling people to go and find a stamp that did not exist in the file they already had. Your tracker is current at v1.6; you do not need a new one.

v1.7  ·  25 Sep 2026

The cone of outcomes, and a card that says what you typed over

A new chart under the cumulative table on Liquidity Outlook. That table is exact and unreadable at a glance — seventeen rows of three columns — and the thing worth taking from it is a shape: how fast the gap between a bad future and a good one opens up. The shaded band runs from the 10th to the 90th percentile with the median through it, there is a dashed line at the capital you committed, and the year each edge crosses that line is stated in words underneath. It is drawn from the same rows the table prints, so the two cannot disagree.

A deal card now says which figures you typed over the shared file. Your value is still the one that runs, and that has not changed. What has changed is that the disagreement is visible. A number you chose on purpose and a number that went stale when a sponsor’s terms were corrected used to look exactly alike — which is how a 5-to-8 payout window sat under a deal whose documents say 6 to 8 for a day. Both figures now print, yours first.

Just say whether it is a fund

There is a new column in the tracker called Fund or Single deal. Type one or the other and the tool stops guessing. A fund sells down over several years; a single company or property is bought on one date, and that difference changes when your money shows up.

It was possible to express this before by counting the deals inside a fund, which was an indirect way to ask a question you already know the answer to. The count still matters for one thing only: a venture fund of 20 or more is priced differently from one company.

Bug sweep

The new version went up fast, so it has been put through a full test pass. Two real problems turned up and both are fixed.

A short deal name could pick up the wrong terms. A position named with just a couple of letters would quietly match a shared deal file that happened to start the same way, and inherit its sponsor, hold and multiple without saying so. If you bookmarked this as the Exit Timing Calendar, nothing is lost and the link is unchanged. The calendar is now the Liquidity Outlook tab, and there are two more tabs beside it. The page opens on the new one, so this is the change you are noticing.

v1.2  ·  24 Sep 2026

Funds no longer need you to count their deals

In v1.1 a fund only paid out over several years if you told it how many investments it held. That was the wrong question. Whether a fund sells down over time has nothing to do with how many things are in it — it is simply what funds do.

So anything paying a preferred return or a preferred return is now treated as a fund automatically, and pays out over a window, with nothing to fill in. If you hold a single company that pays a preferred return, put 1 in the Deals in fund column and it will exit on one date instead, the way a company being bought actually happens.

You can tell it what your sponsor told you

There are two new optional columns, Liquidity from and Liquidity to, counted in years from when you funded the deal. If a sponsor has said “expect distributions in years five through eight”, put 5 and 8 in those columns and that is exactly what the tool will use.

Leave them blank and it assumes the sell-down runs over the back 40% of the hold, which is roughly what most sponsors describe anyway. Worth knowing: a fund’s pay-out window and its stated life are not the same thing. A property fund can finish selling in year six and formally wind up in year seven.

The deal count still matters for one thing only: a venture fund holding 20 or more companies is priced differently from a single company. That has not changed.

v1.1  ·  24 Sep 2026

Funds are no longer treated like single deals

A fund that holds twenty or thirty companies behaves nothing like a single company, and the first version priced them the same way. The old maths gave every venture position a 30% chance of returning nothing. For one company that is fair. For a fund of twenty it would take every company in it failing at once, which has never happened to a real fund.

Funds now use their own set of odds, taken from what venture funds have actually returned rather than from anything invented here. A useful side effect: it puts the sponsor’s own target multiple at roughly the top 5% of outcomes, which is where a target belongs. Something to aim at, not the expected result.

Funds pay out over several years, not all on one day

A fund sells its holdings gradually, so the cash arrives over a stretch of years. The tool used to drop the whole lot into a single year, which for something meant to be a calendar was the bigger mistake of the two. Anything holding more than one deal now spreads its payout across the years around its exit.

This moves no money. It only puts it in the right years, and your lifetime totals come out exactly the same either way.

What you need to do

There is a new column in the tracker called Deals in fund. Put 1 there, or leave it empty, and nothing changes from before. Put the real number in and your funds stop being priced like single companies. If you are not sure how many the fund holds, ask the sponsor.

A single company being bought is still one event on one date, so leave those at 1.
