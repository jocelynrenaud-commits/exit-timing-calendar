Private Deals

Everything you hold in private markets, and when it might pay you back. The Liquidity Outlook tab is the Exit Timing Calendar, now part of a bigger tool. It will still not give you a predicted exit date, because nobody has one.

What’s new

Every time the maths behind this tool changes, the version at the bottom of the page changes with it. It is worth knowing which one you were looking at, because two answers produced by different versions are not directly comparable.

v1.7  ·  25 Sep 2026

The cone of outcomes, and a card that says what you typed over

A new chart under the cumulative table on Liquidity Outlook. That table is exact and unreadable at a glance — seventeen rows of three columns — and the thing worth taking from it is a shape: how fast the gap between a bad future and a good one opens up. The shaded band runs from the 10th to the 90th percentile with the median through it, there is a dashed line at the capital you committed, and the year each edge crosses that line is stated in words underneath. It is drawn from the same rows the table prints, so the two cannot disagree.

A deal card now says which figures you typed over the shared file. Your value is still the one that runs, and that has not changed. What has changed is that the disagreement is visible. A number you chose on purpose and a number that went stale when a sponsor’s terms were corrected used to look exactly alike — which is how a 5-to-8 payout window sat under a deal whose documents say 6 to 8 for a day. Both figures now print, yours first.

v1.6  ·  24 Sep 2026

Asilia, rebuilt from the actual offering documents

The shared Asilia terms have been replaced from the April 2026 presentation, the Growth Circle refreshed analysis, the underwrite and the Q&A notes. The base case is 5.27x over 8 years, not the 3.27x that was here. The fund runs 5 years of deployment then a 3-year harvest with no extension, so the equity kickers land in years 6 to 8 rather than 5 to 8. Fees are 1.3% and 30% carry under $400k, 1.2% and 20% above it.

Asilia Credit Fund Evergreen is its own file now

A holder pointed out that ACFE and the Asilia GC Fund were being run together. They are different vehicles. ACFE is the conservative sleeve on its own: senior secured lending, evergreen, no wind-down and no exit multiple to quote. The GC Fund is the three-sleeve wrapper that holds it alongside Walnut and Opportunistic. If you hold the standalone, use the ACFE file.

An ambiguous name now matches nothing

Typing just “Asilia” used to attach whichever file had the longer name. Two vehicles start with that word, so the tool would have picked one and looked confident about it. When more than one deal could match, none is used and you supply your own terms. Writing the fuller name resolves it.

v1.5  ·  24 Sep 2026

Just say whether it is a fund

There is a new column in the tracker called Fund or Single deal. Type one or the other and the tool stops guessing. A fund sells down over several years; a single company or property is bought on one date, and that difference changes when your money shows up.

It was possible to express this before by counting the deals inside a fund, which was an indirect way to ask a question you already know the answer to. The count still matters for one thing only: a venture fund of 20 or more is priced differently from one company.

Asilia’s shared terms are being checked

A holder pointed out that the terms in the shared Asilia file came from the first offering and do not match the current one, and that Asilia Credit Evergreen Fund is a separate vehicle with one sleeve, no exit and a high-teens annual return. Her documents, not mine. The card now says so rather than presenting terms that may be wrong.

This is exactly what shared files are supposed to do: put the terms where enough people can see them that somebody spots the error. If a shared file disagrees with your own paperwork, type over it. Your numbers always win.

v1.4  ·  24 Sep 2026

Bug sweep

The new version went up fast, so it has been put through a full test pass. Two real problems turned up and both are fixed.

A short deal name could pick up the wrong terms. A position named with just a couple of letters would quietly match a shared deal file that happened to start the same way, and inherit its sponsor, hold and multiple without saying so. A partial match now needs a proper amount of the name to agree. An exact name still matches at any length.

An apostrophe or a quotation mark in a deal name broke its card. The card would not open, and any angle brackets in the name were treated as formatting. Names with punctuation now work normally.

Neither changed any number in anyone’s results. If you find anything else, please say so.

v1.3  ·  24 Sep 2026

Same link, bigger tool — and it opens somewhere new

If you bookmarked this as the Exit Timing Calendar, nothing is lost and the link is unchanged. The calendar is now the Liquidity Outlook tab, and there are two more tabs beside it. The page opens on the new one, so this is the change you are noticing.

Private Deal Dashboard

Everything you hold in one place: committed, funded, still callable, allocation, and a card per deal you can open for its terms. At the top, two multiples side by side — what the sponsors project, and what the same deals are expected to return once the outcomes that go nowhere are weighted in. Most tools show you only the first.

There is also a year-by-year table of capital calls going out against preferred income coming in. Exits are deliberately not on it: a coupon is promised and an exit is hoped for, and adding them together is the most misleading thing one of these tools can do. Exits stay on the Liquidity Outlook tab.

You can leave most of the tracker blank now

The tool ships with a small shared file for each of the common GC deals, carrying the sponsor, the vehicle, the structure, the preferred rate, the hold, the fees and when the sponsor says it pays out. If your deal name matches one, all of that fills itself in and you only supply your numbers: what you committed, what you have funded, and when.

Anything you type wins over the shared file. It only fills what you left empty and never argues with you. A deal nobody has shared still works exactly as before.

Nothing personal is in a shared file. There is a new Thesis column for why you did the deal, and that stays in your spreadsheet, on your machine, like everything else.

Background

The assumptions, the rules the tool enforces, and a plain list of what it cannot tell you. Worth two minutes before you trust any of the numbers.

v1.2  ·  24 Sep 2026

Funds no longer need you to count their deals

In v1.1 a fund only paid out over several years if you told it how many investments it held. That was the wrong question. Whether a fund sells down over time has nothing to do with how many things are in it — it is simply what funds do.

So anything paying a preferred return or a coupon is now treated as a fund automatically, and pays out over a window, with nothing to fill in. If you hold a single company that pays a preferred return, put 1 in the Deals in fund column and it will exit on one date instead, the way a company being bought actually happens.

You can tell it what your sponsor told you

There are two new optional columns, Liquidity from and Liquidity to, counted in years from when you funded the deal. If a sponsor has said “expect distributions in years five through eight”, put 5 and 8 in those columns and that is exactly what the tool will use.

Leave them blank and it assumes the sell-down runs over the back 40% of the hold, which is roughly what most sponsors describe anyway. Worth knowing: a fund’s pay-out window and its stated life are not the same thing. A property fund can finish selling in year six and formally wind up in year seven.

The deal count still matters for one thing only: a venture fund holding 20 or more companies is priced differently from a single company. That has not changed.

v1.1  ·  24 Sep 2026

Funds are no longer treated like single deals

A fund that holds twenty or thirty companies behaves nothing like a single company, and the first version priced them the same way. The old maths gave every venture position a 30% chance of returning nothing. For one company that is fair. For a fund of twenty it would take every company in it failing at once, which has never happened to a real fund.

Funds now use their own set of odds, taken from what venture funds have actually returned rather than from anything invented here. A useful side effect: it puts the sponsor’s own target multiple at roughly the top 5% of outcomes, which is where a target belongs. Something to aim at, not the expected result.

Funds pay out over several years, not all on one day

A fund sells its holdings gradually, so the cash arrives over a stretch of years. The tool used to drop the whole lot into a single year, which for something meant to be a calendar was the bigger mistake of the two. Anything holding more than one deal now spreads its payout across the years around its exit.

This moves no money. It only puts it in the right years, and your lifetime totals come out exactly the same either way.

What you need to do

There is a new column in the tracker called Deals in fund. Put 1 there, or leave it empty, and nothing changes from before. Put the real number in and your funds stop being priced like single companies. If you are not sure how many the fund holds, ask the sponsor.

A single company being bought is still one event on one date, so leave those at 1.
